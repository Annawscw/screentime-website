The Facts

It's not a willpower problem.

You are not competing against yourself. You are competing against teams of very well-paid engineers whose entire job is to make sure you keep scrolling. Here's how they do it.

Why it's hard to stop

The pull-to-refresh is a pokie machine

If a reward comes every time, you get bored of it. If it comes sometimes — unpredictably — you keep pulling. Psychologists call this a variable-ratio reward schedule, and it is the single most powerful way known to make a behaviour stick. It is also exactly how a slot machine works, and exactly how the refresh gesture on every social app works. That is not an accident; the gesture was designed on purpose.

Infinite scroll removes every stopping point

Ten years ago you finished a page and had to click "next". That click was a moment where your brain got to ask "do I want to keep doing this?" Infinite scroll deleted that moment. The person who invented it has publicly said he regrets it.

Streaks turn a friendship into an obligation

A streak isn't a measure of how close you are to someone. It's a number that costs you nothing to keep and feels bad to lose — which is precisely why it exists. Loss feels roughly twice as bad as an equivalent gain feels good, and streaks are built directly on top of that.

Autoplay decides for you

The next video starts before you've decided you want it. By the time you notice, you're 20 seconds in and quitting now feels like wasting those 20 seconds. Turning autoplay off is a two-tap change that gives you back the decision.

If it were about willpower, the apps wouldn't need a thousand engineers.

What it does to sleep

Blue light gets all the attention, but it's the smaller of the two problems. The bigger one is arousal and displacement: an argument in the comments, a cliffhanger, a group chat going off — that keeps your brain switched on. And every "five more minutes" pushes your actual bedtime later, night after night, until you're running a permanent sleep debt through the school week.

Teenagers need roughly 8–10 hours. Adolescent body clocks also shift later naturally — so you're already fighting biology before the phone gets involved.

Not all screen time is equal

This matters, and most "screen time bad" messaging gets it wrong. Three hours is not three hours:

  • Creating — editing, making music, coding, writing. Generally fine, often great.
  • Connecting — actually talking to people you know. Broadly neutral to positive.
  • Consuming — passive, endless, algorithm-fed scrolling. This is the one linked to feeling worse.

So don't aim for "less screen time". Aim for less of the third one.

The school phone ban

Since Term 2 2024 every state school in New Zealand has had to require phones to be away for the whole school day. The Education Review Office's follow-up review found most schools reported students talking to each other more at break times and fewer classroom distractions — but also that compliance depended heavily on whether schools enforced it consistently. Whatever you think of it, it's the clearest signal you'll get that this isn't only a teenagers-being-lazy story: adults concluded the environment itself was the problem.
